Source: the SOIC video "I Studied 7 Indian Bull Runs. The Leaders Kept Changing" (transcript recorded in 2026).

## 1. Why rotation happens: two charts, same stocks

- Between about 2010 and 2019, a high-ROCE consumer business (a leading paint company) had a phenomenal run, while a cyclical, low-ROCE capital-goods/power-equipment business (BHEL) sat poor, with weak thermal-power demand and negative ROCE.
- After 2019/2020, the cyclical company's ROCE moved from negative to about 8-10% and its stock **outperformed the paint company** over the later period, even though the paint company remained a 20%+ ROCE business.
- Lesson: the same two stocks can swap leadership as cycles turn; quality of business does not protect from de-rating after a long run.
- Another example: two graphite-electrode companies saw a massive boom in 2017-18 when top line and bottom line surged within a year; the stock prices then fell back for years (short-dated cycle).

## 2. The bull-run timeline

The video describes **seven bull-run phases since 1999: six completed and a seventh still forming**, each led by a different cluster of sectors. (This count is my reading of the narration.)

| # | Period | Leaders | How it ended / notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1999-2003 (approx.) | IT, telecom, media (information and communications tech) | Dot-com bust and a market scam. The broad index more than doubled in about 14-15 months, then fell about 51% over the next ~19 months; many IT/tech index stocks fell 84-98%. |
| 2 | 2003-2009 | **Capex super-cycle**: capital goods, power, real estate, metals | Heavy winners (for example, a leading engineering company about +730%, a power-equipment company about +580%, others around +500-600% between 2005 and 2008); the index PE reached about 28x. The bust (global financial crisis) saw leaders fall 48-82% and the index fall ~58%. A defensive consumer giant expected to lead afterwards only rose about 76% in three to four years and was down just ~4% in the bust. |
| 3 | 2009-2013 | **Stimulus, then safety**: autos, banks rebounded; then FMCG, pharma, IT | Slower run; about a 28% correction at the end. The previous run had been ferocious, so this one looked slow by comparison. |
| 4 | 2013-2020 | **Financialisation**: private banks, NBFCs, consumer brands, autos, small caps | Examples: a leading NBFC about +1,400% in five years, its holding company about +760%, an auto leader about +600%, an apparel/innerwear company reaching a very high share price; the index about +111%. A bank with a fraud collapse about +600% then collapsed. Ended with a credit crisis (IL&FS) and a small-cap collapse; a few large-caps kept rising until COVID took the market down ~38%. |
| 5 | Mar 2020-Oct 2021 | **COVID bull run**: metals, IT, new-age tech, pharma | Metals index up strongly; IT and engineering-R&D names surged; some pharma/diagnostic names also boomed because demand was boosted by COVID. The index later fell about 17%, and many stocks were "taken to the cleaners". |
| 6 | Jun 2022-Mar 2025 | **PSUs, defence, capex/capital goods**, real estate, PSU banks, autos | Defence index about +333% (Jun 2022-Sep 2024); a CPSE index and realty about 2x; PSU banks, autos around 1.5x; the broad index about +71%. The correction was driven by elections, higher taxes and high valuations. |
| 7 (current) | From the Mar 2025 low | Capital markets, PSU banks, defence, autos, pockets of pharma | The speaker said this is not yet a "proper" bull run. |

## 3. After-the-bust leadership pattern (the "pattern passes on")

Observed in the Indian market:

- After the 2003-08 capex and metals bust → **FMCG** led (a leading FMCG company about +100% in 2015-16).
- After the 2015-16 shakeout → **pharma** did well, later fell in turn.
- After the 2018-20 credit bust → **IT** led the 2020 rally.
- After the 2022 reset → **PSU** index led.
- After the 2024-25 correction → **financial services** led, in line with the index.
- After a 2026 fall → **pharma** was leading.
- Sectors that did very badly between 2010 and 2019 were leaders after 2020; sectors that did badly after 2020 might make a comeback in 2025-26.

Generalised lesson: when a bust ends, look first at the sectors that were ignored or held up well; the leaders of the last boom rarely lead the next.

## 4. Psychology examples

- **Internet-platform IPO cluster:** several internet/new-age companies (payments, food/quick delivery, insurance/financial marketplace, beauty e-commerce) listed close together when private-equity holders wanted to sell. The payments company, at the time of the video, still had not crossed its post-IPO price. A related example: one internet company raised a large QIP when its PE was 110-130x and today trades at ~15-20x, and used QIP money for buybacks (euphoric-time raise, despair-time buyback). The company's name was garbled in the transcript.
- **Solar:** from 2021-24 many unlisted solar companies rose 5x-30x in three years because government liquidity and policy and investor money poured in; a cluster of IPOs followed in late 2024 and 2025.
- **Agrochemicals:** several domestic formulation companies IPO'd in 2021-22; the sector never regained those valuations.
- **Space and AI (US, per the speaker):** after a very large space-company IPO, some space stocks were reported down 70-90% (they traded on price-to-sales); the speaker suggested the timing of a major AI-lab IPO needed a massive AI bull market first.
- **Breadth peak:** in January 2024 roughly 90-95% of the listed universe was above the 200-day EMA.
- **Narrative:** "infrastructure decade" and "India's century" were commonly repeated, including during the 2024 elections; by 2026 almost nobody was saying them, with doubts about government policy and trade.
- **Parabolic assets:** a precious metal (silver) ran parabolically from December to March; "it will hit ₹1 lakh" was the common sentiment, after which people stopped talking about it. Several Indian stocks and themes were parabolic in 2021 (a rail-ticketing company, a power exchange, a few chemicals names).

## 5. Capital-cycle examples

- **Solar manufacturing:** the industry earned 30-40% ROCE at the peak; several companies IPO'd in 2024-25; capital poured in. Abroad (China, US) companies went bankrupt on oversupply; in India, the end state of oversupply would be insolvency proceedings.
- **Smart meters:** big orders in 2024 invited new entrants, including a large public-sector firm and a major conglomerate that backward-integrated through an acquisition; unlisted players expanded too.
- **Transformers:** multiple smaller companies IPO'd over the past two years; a large amount of planned capacity; margins at several leaders slipping (for example, from about 19.5% to 15.5% for one, and from about 31% to 20% for another, over two to three quarters). Distribution transformers were at risk of oversupply. The speaker's view was that many companies' profits might revert toward 12-14% margins within 12-18 months.
- **Cables and wires:** the custom sector index looked ready to enter Stage 4, with competition intensifying even though returns had not yet visibly deteriorated.
- **Paints:** a new competitor entered; small players' returns collapsed; consolidation among a few established players. The index made lower highs since its peak and was only recently stabilising.
- **Coworking spaces:** an oversupply of capital (many IPOs, new entrants) was flagged as a possible bad-cycle candidate.
- **Water EPC:** government payment delays and an IPO wave left the sector down about 42-43%.
- **Diagnostics:** see section 7.
- **Hotels:** at the time, in a high-returns phase (15-20% ROCE) after a very long lull.

## 6. Valuation-bubble examples (slow, painful unwinds)

- **Retail/consumer brands (2017-19):** a set of consistent earners (a retail supermarket chain, paints, adhesives, a pipes company and others) traded at 100-200x earnings. Over the following five years, price returns were negative (about -31%, -28%, -23%, -18%, -15% for several of them) while the consumer staples index was up only about 23%.
- **A fashion retailer (2024):** traded at about 144x earnings and about 20x sales while growth decelerated from 50-70% to 39, 34, 27, 19, 15 and about 20%. The speaker said he would question the valuation as the rate of change slowed.
- **A supermarket chain:** EPS about +20% over five years but price about -7% on a five-year basis (absolute return about -31% in the speaker's example); the multiple fell from about 226x to about 73x. Slower growth and changing consumption patterns (quick commerce took higher-spending customers) were cited. It took about five to six years for the de-rating, and it was not "the worst company in the world" for a retailer.

Lesson: a valuation-led correction takes years, is painful for holders, and happens even when profits grow.

## 7. Earnings-bubble examples

- **Diagnostics (2020-22):** after the COVID spike, a diagnostics index went from about ₹22 to about ₹82 (index units) in about 18 months; many stocks rose 4-6x. Private equity and platform entrants (including a leading e-pharmacy) entered; pricing, PE and margins collapsed; the index fell to the 39 range. Top-line growth had been 19-32% and then normalised toward 10-15%, only recently recovering to the mid-teens as pricing power returned. An earnings-led bubble: 4-5 years of growth compressed into a year or two.
- **AI hardware (speaker's discussion):** a large AI-chip leader trading at a low PE of about 10x (in the transcript) could "evaporate after two years" if the capex-driven demand does not repeat; the point is that low PE on peak earnings can be misleading.

## 9. Tool examples (for the toolkit)

- **Valuation band:** a headline index spent 2018-20 below average PE, 2020-22 above average, 2022-24 expensive, 2024-26 very expensive, and then about 19-20x. With recent profit growth of about 21%, the speaker saw re-rating potential. The IT index had a 3-year median of about 27x, a 5-year median of about 26x, and was about 18x at the time; pre-COVID about 15-20x; peaks of about 40x (2021) and 35x (2024).
- **RS scores:** a capital-markets index around 84, an internet index around 76 (again above 75 after a long gap), a PSU-bank index in the top 6% of the market. Industries repeatedly in the outperforming zone: chlorination, music licensing, watches, exchanges (the speaker's bullish reasons: bottom-up players growing faster, operating leverage, limited players), plus marine port services (policy-led; a dredging company outperforming for two years and a large port company for about 2.5 years).
- **Custom indices:** cable sector, paint sector, diagnostics, contract manufacturing (about +50-60% in six months), hospitals (rebounded after a regulatory scare; Q2 profits improved).
- **Results-season scan:** transformer and smart-meter margins fell together; in B2B/specialty healthcare and similar sub-sectors, order-book intake and profitability growth were rising together.
