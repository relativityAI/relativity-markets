# The Four Drivers of Sectoral Cycles

Sector cycles come from four drivers: **psychology, capital cycle, valuation, earnings**. They often collaborate: for example, a capital flood (driver 2) lifts supply, which hits earnings (driver 4), which resets valuation (driver 3), which shifts psychology (driver 1).

Contents: 1 Psychology | 2 Capital cycle | 3 Valuation-led bubbles | 4 Earnings-led bubbles | 5 Combining the drivers

---

## 1. Psychology-led cycles

When the environment is euphoric, a stock or sector is **priced for perfection**. When it is despairing, multiples collapse and the sector looks cheap versus its own history. Liquidity often creates the fundamentals, so euphoric money flows can themselves manufacture the earnings that appear to justify the price.

### Euphoria signals
- **IPO clustering.** When a sector is near its peak, the largest IPOs and the most IPOs in that sector tend to arrive, often close together in time, as owners (including private equity and venture backers) sell into strength. A wave of IPOs, QIPs/follow-ons or private fundraisings in one sector is a warning that conditions are euphoric.
  - Pattern: multiples are elevated, IPOs arrive one after another, and afterwards the sector often never regains the valuation it had at the IPO window (examples: a cluster of internet-platform IPOs; agrochemical formulation IPOs; a cluster of solar-manufacturer IPOs).
  - Pattern: companies raise equity (QIP) at peak multiples and later use cash for buybacks at trough multiples. Equity raising in euphoria and buybacks in despair mark the two ends of the cycle.
- **Supply of money/liquidity into one theme.** Government stimulus or policy pumping liquidity into a sector, plus investors pouring money into private fundraising, can lift unlisted/early companies several-fold in a few years, then listing waves follow.
- **Parabolic extension.** A price more than ~70% above its 150-day exponential moving average signals euphoria / parabolic stage. This works on any asset class (equities, commodities, indices). See `toolkit.md`.
- **Broad-market breadth.** When ~90-95% of the listed universe trades above its 200-day EMA, the whole market is in a euphoric phase.
- **Narrative.** Look for slogans ("this is the infrastructure decade", "the country's century", "$X is going to Y") repeated everywhere, including during election campaigns. When the narrative fades and people start questioning government policy or the macro story, euphoria has typically passed. After a commodity or sector spike, note that people **stop talking about it**.
- **A friend's anecdote is data about sentiment.** Casual chatter that suddenly changes from "this will go to the moon" to silence is a sentiment shift.

### Despair signals
- Valuations compress and the sector trades at the low end of its own multi-year range while earnings are intact or recovering.
- IPO pipeline dries up; buybacks appear; narrative turns negative.

### What to do with it
Psychology tells you **where in the sentiment cycle** you are. It does not tell you how long the downturn lasts. Pair it with cycle duration (`cycle-duration.md`).

## 2. Capital-cycle-led cycles

Reference: *Capital Returns* (edited by Edward Chancellor, Marathon Asset Management), a collection that develops the capital-cycle model. Suggest it to users who want depth.

### The loop
1. **High returns on capital** in an industry that is commoditised, often because of unusually good times (demand spike, shortage, policy support).
2. **Capital floods in**: new entrants, capacity announcements, big capex, IPOs, private-equity funding, conglomerates backward-integrating.
3. **Capacity overshoots**; returns on capital and margins collapse.
4. **Capital exits** (bankruptcies, consolidation, no new investment); supply tightens.
5. Return to step 1: high returns again.

Label these as capital-cycle *phases* (not to be confused with chart *stages* in `toolkit.md`).

### How to spot each phase
- **Phase 1 (high returns):** industry earning unusually high ROCE (examples from source: solar manufacturing at 30-40% ROCE; hotels at 15-20% ROCE). Few new entrants yet.
- **Phase 2 (capital flooding in):** capex announcements get very large; IPOs by small and mid-sized players in the sector; large groups and public-sector firms entering; unlisted players expanding; private-equity funding. Track **capex spend** of the sector's companies: if capex is very large relative to existing capacity, competition is coming.
- **Phase 3 (overshoot):** margins start slipping across the sector (look at several quarters in a row), raw material or pricing pressure, new capacity visible in industry data, price wars, small players' returns collapse, consolidation among a few established players.
- **Phase 4 (capital exits):** bankruptcies or insolvency proceedings, no new capex for years, shortages emerge, returns start recovering.

### Practical checklist (use 2-3 of these together)
1. **IPO wave** in the sector? (counts, sizes, timing)
2. **Stage analysis** of a custom sector index (basket of constituents). A sector whose index is making lower highs, with only recent signs of stabilisation, is in a late phase.
3. **Capex and capacity data** for the sector (announced vs existing).
4. **Margin trend** across the sector's major companies over 3+ quarters.
5. **New entrants** (large conglomerates, backward integration, PE-funded startups).

### Source-video illustrations (use as mechanisms, not as the user's facts)
- Solar, smart meters, transformers, cables and wires, paints, diagnostics, coworking and water EPC each show capital flooding in, with margin/return pressure following. See `case-studies-india.md`.
- Hotels illustrate a sector currently in high-returns phase after a long lull.

## 3. Valuation-led bubbles ("the future is paid for in advance")

A quality business with a visible runway can get valued as if the whole runway will be delivered flawlessly. When this happens, **the correction is slow and painful**: it is a multi-year compression, not a crash.

### Signature
- Very high PE (and/or very high price-to-sales for lower-margin businesses) relative to the sector's own history and to growth.
- Earnings keep growing, but the **price goes sideways or down for years** as the multiple de-rates (example pattern: earnings per share up strongly over five years while price is down; multiple de-rates from the 200s to ~70s).
- Leaders can underperform the broad index for five years or more even though the underlying business improves.

### The key trigger to watch: rate of change slows
The most important thing to track is **the rate of change of growth**. When top-line growth that ran at 50-70% decays step by step (e.g., to 39, 34, 27, 19, 15 and then ~20%), question the assumption that the multiple will hold. Also watch for:
- **Alternatives or new options for customers** (a new channel or format that changes consumption patterns).
- **Saturation of the expansion runway** (the company or competitors keep adding stores/units with diminishing incremental returns).
- **Three-year profit CAGR** (EBITDA, PAT) dropping well below the longer-run figure.

### Practical rule
If you hold a stock whose multiple embeds a long runway, define an exit rule *based on rate-of-change deceleration over several quarters*, not on the price chart alone. Question whether the re-rating will persist.

### Why consumer stocks are a classic example
Consistent earners (consumer brands, staples, paints, adhesives) that traded at 100-150-200x earnings in a "quality at any price" phase, then delivered flat or negative 5-year price returns even while earnings grew. See `case-studies-india.md` for numbers.

## 4. Earnings-led bubbles ("borrowing from the future")

### Rule of thumb
Industry growth is usually in the range of **2-3x GDP growth**. If an industry grows **5-6x faster than the economy**, it has very likely **borrowed from the growth of the future**.

### Signature
- A 4-5 year growth cycle gets **compressed into 12-18 months**. This happens with hyper-fast growth or non-linear bumper earnings (a shock, a policy window, a one-off demand surge).
- **Low PE at peak earnings.** The multiple looks cheap because the "E" is unsustainably high. Ask: are these earnings sustainable, or could they evaporate? (Example discussed: a capex-driven AI hardware boom where, if the build-out is front-loaded, the earnings from that capex may not repeat once the build-out is done.)
- Capital cycle overlay: very high profits attract competitors, which shrink pricing power (e.g., diagnostics after the COVID spike).

### Questions to ask
- What would revenue and margins look like if this period's extraordinary demand normalised?
- Is there a **new inflection** (a new product, new technology) that could restore growth, or is it a one-off?
- Are earnings recurring or capex-/policy-timing driven?

### After the bubble
Earnings can normalise to well below the spike for a few years, and pricing power may return slowly (e.g., a sector whose top-line growth fell from ~19-32% to ~10-15%, and only later recovered toward the mid-teens as pricing power returned).

## 5. Combining the drivers

Score each driver as Early / Mid / Late (or Bubble / Normal / Depressed) with evidence. Then:

- **Three or four drivers late** → high peak risk. Plan exit/de-risk triggers now.
- **Psychology despairing, valuation compressed, capital cycle in exit phase, earnings recovering** → classic base-building / early-cycle setup. Look for RS and stage confirmation (see `toolkit.md`).
- **Mixed** → state which drivers dominate and what would break the tie. Do not average away real disagreement.

Remember that valuation- and earnings-led corrections can be slow (years), while capital-cycle breaks and psychology flips can be fast.
