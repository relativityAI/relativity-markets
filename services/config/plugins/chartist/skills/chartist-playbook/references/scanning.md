# Scanning System

The discovery engine of the playbook. The podcast's core claim: **scanning is easy; the hard part is what you do after the list appears** — finding the catalysts and the strong themes. Tools make it a 15–30 minute daily habit.

## The near-high scan (core scan)

**Premise:** a strong stock is found near its 52-week high, not near its low. Expecting a future 5x at a 52-week low is mathematically wishful thinking. Demand accumulates → price rises → stock enters the near-high zone → that zone *is* your candidate list.

**Spec (as run in the podcast):**
- Price within **20% of the 52-week high** (widen to 30% only if you can genuinely process more names).
- Market cap > **₹500 crore**.
- Price > **₹20**.
- Liquidity: **~₹5 crore worth of shares traded daily on average over 10–12 days** (so the stock is tradeable, not illiquid).
- Run **daily** — the habit matters as much as the list (see "run it daily" below).

On Chartink/StockScan a ready-made "52-week high within 10%" scan exists; edit it to 20%. StockScan also has a dedicated long-term-breakout scan.

## Run it daily — and watch the *changes*

Daily running gives you two edges that occasional running does not:
1. **Familiarity:** names rarely leave the list for 1.5–2 years at a stretch, so after a few weeks you *know* the residents. 
2. **New-entrant detection:** when 2–4 fresh names appear, something just started moving — that's the early-warning signal, and it's how you catch themes near their start (e.g., auto-ancillary names crowding in week after week).

## Industry grouping = where capital flows

With ~170–180 names from the scan, the fastest read is: **which industry groups contribute the most names?** The top 4–5 industry groups are where capital is flowing. Why: free-market price mechanism — strong businesses attract institutional demand, demand lifts price, price lands the stock near its high. So the scan converts supply-demand into a theme map automatically.

Analogy used in the podcast: in the Premier League's top-10 goal scorers, if 5 play for one club, that club is the strongest. If 16 auto-ancillary names sit in your scan, auto-ancillary is the strong theme — no economist needed.

**Leader behavior:** within a strong theme, the leaders keep leading (they were the first to move and keep moving). The classic beginner mistake is buying the laggard "because it hasn't run yet" — but stocks that already ran keep running, and the laggard is often laggarding for a fundamental reason. Study the leaders.

## Relative strength (RS)

**Definition:** a stock's performance **compared to a benchmark** (Nifty 50 is the default), over a chosen window. If Nifty moves 10% in a month and the stock moves 15%, the stock is relatively strong. It also reads on the downside: Nifty −5%, stock −3% = relatively strong.

**Window selection (this is the practical part):**
- **1 week to 1 month:** lots of false positives — news-driven moves.
- **6–12 months:** you miss the move by the time it shows.
- **1–3 months: the sweet spot** (consistent with published momentum research the podcast cites — e.g., Fama's 2010 backtesting work).
- Weekly RS can also be done visually on a ~26-week (half-year) lookback if you prefer naked-eye chart reading.

**Naked-eye method (recommended by the guest):** open both charts (or compare on Google Finance / TradingView) over the same window and simply look. An index-vs-index example makes it obvious: Kospi up ~90% in a year while Nifty stayed sideways = Korean market holds the relative strength (in that example, because SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron-adjacent memory names dominated the index). No indicator required — and the podcast explicitly warns that confusing **RS** (vs benchmark) with **RSI** (self-comparison oscillator) wastes whole study hours. See SKILL.md's inline table for the RSI distinction and why "overbought" is not a sell signal in this style.

**What RS does to your scan:** the near-high scan *already embeds* relative strength — a stock hugging its 52-week high while the index is weak is by definition relatively strong. That's why one scan does all the work.

## Theme detection shortcuts

- **Scan grouping** (above) — the primary method.
- **Market/theme scans** on StockScan-style tools: auto-ancillaries, gold-loan names, metals (steel/aluminium), PSU banks — if the same theme keeps topping, that's your shortlist.
- **External shocks reshuffle themes.** When a market-wide external shock hits (Covid, tariff headlines), themes reshuffle: the previous leader theme goes dormant and a new theme starts (pharma after 2013–19 dormancy, then railway/defense, then solar, then oil, then gold-loan on the gold move). After every deep correction, look for which theme's names re-enter the scan first.
- **Result-season shortcut:** strong results cluster — when 6–7 names from one sector report blowout quarters in the same season, capital follows profits; "the stock goes where the profit of the company and the industry goes."

## Result-season scan

Track unusually strong quarterly results with a simple screen (podcast's settings):
- **P/E < 50**, **PAT growth > 20%**, **revenue growth > 10%**.
- During result season, check weekly: which of these names have you not studied, and why are the numbers this good? (E.g., a spirits company's multi-year best results after an ethanol turnaround.)
- This is a study feed, not a buy feed — knowing the winners of the season keeps your theme map current.

## Announcement scans (the daily research half)

The guest's habit — beyond near-high names, run standing scans over corporate announcements:
1. **Presentations (PPTs)** — read the latest decks on an iPad in the evening, fast. Even for stocks you don't own: if the sector leader's deck shows slowing same-store/LFL growth, you've learned the sector is under pressure before the followers report.
2. **Con-calls** — management's own framing of the trajectory.
3. **Buybacks** — capital allocation signal (and EPS accretion mechanics).
4. **M&A / merger-demerger announcements** — value unlocking events (podcast examples: Devyani–Sapphire merger talk; Lemon Tree demerging an asset-heavy unit so the asset-light management business gets its own re-rating; Edelweiss → Nuvama).
5. **Order-book updates** — especially for capital-goods/defense names.
6. **Warrants / promoter infusions** — when promoters fund the company personally via warrants, that's conviction visible on the exchange (podcast example: an Exicom-style promoter infusion).
7. **Monthly business updates** — NBFC/bank monthly numbers (podcast examples: CSB, FedFina, Ujjivan, Ujjivan SFB updates). Crucially, business updates published before earnings give you **guidance in advance** — you no longer have to guess whether the earnings will be good or bad.
8. **Credit-rating reports** — dense but gold; the guest's trick: paste the PDF into an AI and ask for plain-language simplification. AI has genuinely expanded everyone's circle of competence by making complicated businesses understandable ("explain it to me like I'm a kid").

Total honest cost: ~1.5–2 hours a day including near-high study (scanning itself: 15–30 minutes).

## The daily research protocol for a shortlisted stock

1. **Company website** — what does this business actually sell, how does it make money? (Works for visual learners; the podcast guest explicitly runs on pictures.)
2. **Quick financials** — margin profile, PAT, revenue — briefly, to see the shape of the business.
3. **Latest PPT + latest con-call** — where does management say the growth comes from? Now the chart makes sense: *"that's why the buying."*
4. **Skip the deep annual-report dig unless you need it** — the technicals already told you *that* buying is happening; fundamentals tell you *why*, so you can rank candidates against each other (e.g., Lumax 3x'd while a comparable peer barely moved — the difference is in the business).

## The guest's actual daily schedule (for realism)

- ~30 min scanning; 3.5 hours evening study (5–9pm); 3–4 hours morning study (4–8am) — study happens *outside market hours*; market hours are for not fiddling.

## Scan-output hygiene

- Keep the list. Don't buy from it reflexively — the list is a radar, the work is the catalyst hunt.
- When a name has been near the high for weeks consolidating (e.g., 12 weeks for an Ashok Leyland before its move), you were given months of free study time — use it, so the trigger day finds you prepared instead of FOMOing.
