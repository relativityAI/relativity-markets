# Fundamental Catalysts — The Taxonomy

Why does a stock move? Because **the profit of the company and the industry goes somewhere, and the stock follows**. This file is the "WHAT to buy" layer: how to find the growth driver behind a strong chart, and how to disqualify a fake one.

## First principles: the P&L ladder (teach this whenever a user asks "where does growth come from?")

Sales → (− raw material cost) = **Gross profit** → (− SG&A: electricity, employees, rent) = **EBITDA** → (− depreciation & amortization) = **EBIT** → (− interest) = **PBT** → (− tax) = **PAT**.

To grow PAT you must pull one of these levers — and every catalyst below maps onto this ladder. This mental model makes any company's growth story auditable in five minutes.

## The catalyst taxonomy (with podcast examples)

### Growth of the top line
1. **Volume growth** — selling more units (10 chip packets → 12).
2. **Value/realization growth** — same volume, higher price (₹20 packet → ₹30). Premiumization is the quiet version of this.
3. **New product introduction** — new SKUs, new categories.
4. **New brand introduction** — a fresh consumer-facing identity (Zudio inside Trent; Titan entering new categories; Mahindra's EV-born brands XUV 9e etc.). Brand launches reset growth ceilings.
5. **Geographical expansion** — moving into new territories (a jeweller opening its first store in a new metro).
6. **Distribution expansion** — more touchpoints (a beverage maker adding tens of thousands of retailers; an auto-parts maker adding capacity towns). Scale in reach, not just product.

### Growth of margins & scale
7. **Operating leverage** — the strongest mental model of the episode: costs were already incurred (factory built, people hired, power running) and **demand arrives** (post-Covid, a GST cut, CV-cycle turn, gold-price boom for gold-loan books) → costs spread over a bigger sales base → EBITDA margin explodes. Examples: a polycab-type cables story, a beverage major, the CV cycle, **NVIDIA** going from ~7% to ~63% operating margin on industry demand explosion.
8. **Economies of scale** — gross margin improves as volume compounds.
9. **Premiumization / content-per-unit growth** — the value of the company's content per vehicle/kit rises (auto-aesthetics and auto-electricals names whose kit value per vehicle kept climbing as dashboards became three-screen cockpits).
10. **Value-added product transition** — a cash cow funds the birth of a cash engine: a film company moving from commodity polyester to sun-control/protection films; an engineering firm seeding an aerospace/defense/semiconductor line that was <₹50cr once; a coffee company's capex commercializing.
11. **Cost reduction via backward integration** — making in-house what was bought outside (a fertilizer company's ammonia integration).

### Below-the-line & balance-sheet catalysts
12. **De-leveraging / interest-cost reduction** — profitable company with old expensive debt repays or refinances: interest cost from 18.5% to 12.5% after restructuring, or a wire-ropes maker renegotiating with lenders (convert some debt to equity, forgive some interest, repay the rest) as the cycle turned.
13. **Corporate restructuring** — selling the wrong business: a steel-wires company selling its steel arm to a conglomerate and turning multi-bagger; focus as a catalyst.
14. **Share buybacks** — ₹100 profit over 100 shares = ₹1 EPS; buy back shares over years, EPS becomes ₹2 **with zero growth**. Notable version: a promoter announcing a buyback at the bottom without participating, raising EPS for everyone and his own holding % — when good times returned, the P/E re-rated instantly. Capital-allocation-literate promoters get rewarded.
15. **Change of management/ownership** — a capable balance sheet buying a capable store (credit rating improves, financing cost drops). Example: a finance company taking on a big global institutional shareholder.

### Industry & system catalysts
16. **Order-book wins** — defense budgets, Kavach train-collision-avoidance systems, solar pump orders. The "sword-maker" story: one buyer's decade of orders = the supplier's decade of growth.
17. **Regulatory change / new norms** — AC cabins mandated in trucks; advanced braking on bikes; instrument clusters and cover glass in EVs; mandatory upgrades create whole new addressable markets for one or two qualified suppliers.
18. **Regulatory approvals / clearances** — mining capacity approvals that triple production; approvals in heavily-regulated industries are binary catalysts.
19. **New industry creation** — an industry that didn't exist (EV componentry, data-center power) starts printing demand.
20. **Industry consolidation** — 16 telecom operators → 2 survivors: the winners inherit pricing power and share (consolidation via price wars can also *be* the catalyst, painfully).
21. **Demand-supply imbalance** — the repeatable pattern: a supply shock or demand explosion creates 1–3 years of extraordinary pricing (shrimp exports 2016-19 at 30–35% CAGR; memory-chip prices +70–80%; optical switches). **These decay.** Trade them, don't marry them.
22. **Growth in the user industry** — your customer's boom is your boom (aerospace demand pulling precision engineering; AI pulling power/cooling).
23. **Client mining** — winning more business from the same client (a ₹800M client → add a second big OEM) and cross-selling new components to existing accounts.
24. **Market-share capture** — industry grows *and* you take share (an SUV maker's share going from ~20% to 35%+ on new launches; a foundry holding the world's only 2nm-capacity fab with its plant fully booked).
25. **Turnarounds** — ETHANOL-type shifts: a distiller's biofuel pivot producing the best results in years. Technicals flag the chart; fundamentals name the turnaround.

### Corporate-action catalysts (events, not operations)
26. **M&A / acquisitions** — buying growth (₹60 cr shop buys a ₹40 cr shop).
27. **Demergers / spin-offs / value unlocking** — a parent's market cap smaller than its spun-off child (an ₹89,000cr parent vs ₹26,000cr demerged wealth arm); five-way conglomerate splits; asset-light management businesses being separated from asset-heavy hotel SPVs so each can get its own multiple.

## Negative triggers — when the catalyst is a trap

These should veto a name regardless of chart strength:

1. **Inflated PAT, negative cash flow, years running** — profit that never becomes cash (a contraceptives company at ~240x P/E with negative operating cash flow). If the P&L and the cash flow disagree for 3–4 years, believe the cash flow.
2. **Everything-promises order books** — an EMS company claiming rail-Kavach *and* smart meters *and* everything else while generating ~₹1 of cash flow in five years. The chart re-rated, then reverted. Products that don't exist can't compound.
3. **Pumped valuations with visible story saturation** — 100x P/E consumer/infrastructure stories at peak narrative (IRCTC/DMart/10,000-P/E hardware names). Fine to *hold* with a trailing stop; terrible to *initiate* 12 months late (late-stage base).
4. **High-base calendar risk** — order-book businesses post-supply-peak will *print* de-growth; know the wagon math before buying the dip.
5. **Fraud signatures** — vertical rise + auditor churn/lender stress + zero cash conversion. Up the staircase, down the elevator.
6. **The laggard trap** — buying the weak sibling of a hot theme because "it hasn't moved yet." Moving is information; the leader's move *is* the theme's proof, and laggards lag for reasons the P&L usually explains.

## How to run the catalyst check in practice (from the guest's routine)

1. Open the **website** → what do they sell, who buys it, how do they make money?
2. Brief **financials** → margin profile, PAT, revenue trajectory.
3. **Latest PPT + con-call** → what does management say is the growth engine? Match it against the taxonomy above — it will usually reduce to 1–2 levers. ("Algebra always simplifies at the end.")
4. For regulated/lending names: **credit-rating reports** (paste into AI for a plain-language summary — the guest's standard move).
5. Ask the differentiating question: *why is this candidate moving while its peer didn't?* The answer is usually the catalyst — and it's how you rank names within a theme.

## Framing discipline

- A catalyst you **cannot state in one sentence** means you haven't found it yet — keep reading, or don't take the position.
- Catalyst + stage + RS must agree. A great catalyst in a stage-4 chart is still a "wait." A stage-2 chart with no nameable catalyst is a "why?" — go find it in the PPT.
